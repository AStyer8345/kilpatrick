Should I Use the Builder’s Preferred Lender When Buying a New Home in Austin?

By Crystal Kilpatrick · November 23, 2026

You do not have to use a builder’s preferred lender, but that lender may offer a package worth considering. The smart comparison is not just the advertised rate or closing-cost credit—it is the full Loan Estimate, the incentive terms, your cash to close, and the payment after any temporary buydown ends.

This comes up constantly when buyers tour new construction in Austin, Dripping Springs, Cedar Park, or farther out in the Hill Country. The sales team may have a rate, a credit, or an upgrade package tied to a particular lender. That can be a real benefit. It can also make it very easy to compare only the headline number instead of the whole loan. Before you decide, get the details in writing and put the offers next to each other.

Why this question matters in the current Central Texas market

Unlock MLS’s October 2026 report for the Austin-Round Rock-San Marcos MSA said affordability continued to shape buyer activity and noted that resale homes were becoming more competitive with new construction. The same report showed 6.2 months of inventory across the metro area. That does not mean every builder or community is offering the same deal. It does mean buyers have a reason to compare a new home’s total cost with other new and resale options instead of deciding from a rate sign in a sales office.

A lender incentive can be useful, especially when it offsets a meaningful portion of closing costs. But it needs to be evaluated against the home price, any required use of the builder’s lender or title company, the loan terms, the closing date, and the homes you could buy nearby for the same monthly budget.

You can shop for your mortgage

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says that even when a homebuilder has a preferred lender, buyers should still look for a better deal. Ask the builder’s lender for a Loan Estimate, then request comparable Loan Estimates from other lenders at roughly the same time. The CFPB recommends comparing multiple offers; its mortgage-shopping guidance suggests aiming for at least three.

Keep the comparison consistent. Use the same purchase price, down payment, loan type, occupancy, and rate-lock period. If one lender is quoting a temporary buydown and another is quoting a permanent rate, ask both to show the payment in every year—not just the first year. A lower payment today can be helpful, but you need to know the payment when the buydown ends.

What to compare besides the interest rate

Start with the Loan Estimate. Look at the interest rate, points, lender credits, origination charges, mortgage insurance, prepaids, total cash to close, and the projected monthly payment. Then separate the builder incentive from the loan itself. Is it a closing-cost credit, a price reduction, an upgrade allowance, or a rate buydown? Does it require a particular loan product or closing date? Is it available on the exact home you want?

I also want buyers to ask what changes if they use an outside lender. Sometimes the difference is small. Sometimes the credit is large enough that the builder’s lender still wins after a full comparison. Either result is fine—as long as it is supported by the numbers, not just a verbal promise or an attractive introductory rate.

New construction has a few extra timing issues

A quick move-in home and a to-be-built home can create different financing decisions. With a completed or near-completed home, the builder may have a firm closing window and an incentive that expires if you miss it. With a longer build, you may need to discuss how long a rate lock lasts, what a lock extension costs, and what happens if the completion date moves. Those terms are property- and lender-specific, so get them in writing before you rely on them.

This is also why I encourage buyers to bring their agent into the conversation early. We can help organize the questions for the builder, lender, title company, and inspector, while your lender explains the loan. The goal is not to make the builder’s lender lose; it is to make sure you choose the package that actually fits your purchase and your life after closing.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to use the builder’s preferred lender?

No. A builder may tie a credit or other incentive to its preferred lender, but the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says you can shop for a mortgage and compare other offers. Ask exactly which incentive terms change if you choose another lender.

How many mortgage offers should I compare for a new construction home?

The CFPB recommends comparing multiple Loan Estimates and suggests aiming for at least three offers. Request estimates using the same purchase price, down payment, loan type, and rate-lock period so the comparison is meaningful.

Is a builder rate buydown always better than a price reduction?

Not always. A buydown may lower your early payment, while a price reduction changes the loan amount. Compare your cash to close, payment during and after any temporary buydown, and the total loan costs before deciding.

What should I ask before accepting a new-home lender incentive?

Ask what the incentive covers, whether it applies to your exact home and loan program, what closing date is required, whether you must use a particular lender or title company, and what happens if the home is delayed. Get each answer in writing.
