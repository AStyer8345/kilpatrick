Buying Near Dripping Springs: What Should You Check About a Well and Septic System?

By Crystal Kilpatrick · November 13, 2026

Before buying a home near Dripping Springs with a private well or septic system, confirm how the property gets water and handles wastewater, then arrange the right specialist evaluations before your contract deadlines. A mailing address or a large lot will not tell you which systems serve the home—or whether they fit your plans.

An acreage home can look like the right fit until you start asking about a future guest house, a pool, or the work involved in maintaining the property. Around Dripping Springs, utility arrangements can vary from one property to the next. I would put those questions near the top of your buying checklist, while you still have time to investigate them.

Start with the address, not the ZIP code

A Dripping Springs mailing address does not establish that a property is inside city limits or connected to public water and sewer. Our 78620 neighborhood guide describes a mix of city and unincorporated locations with different utility arrangements. Confirm the actual water source, wastewater system, service providers, and permitting authority for the address you are considering.

Ask for utility bills, the seller’s disclosure, available permits, system plans, and service records early. If a listing says “water available,” ask what is connected and operating today, and what a connection would require. Do not budget around an assumed hookup.

For a private well, separate water quality from water supply

Water quality and the amount of water a well can provide are different questions. Ask a qualified well professional which inspections, performance checks, and records are appropriate for this property. Ask a qualified laboratory how to collect and test a water sample, and what the results do—and do not—tell you.

Request available drilling records, repair history, and details about pumps, storage, and treatment equipment. Ask about known interruptions or changes in performance. A single test cannot promise future supply, so discuss the limits of any evaluation and the ongoing maintenance the system needs.

If you are planning a pool, more landscaping, or another dwelling, describe that intended use to the professionals evaluating the property. Confirm any applicable groundwater district requirements for the address rather than assuming a nearby property follows the same rules.

For septic, match the records to the house and your plans

Ask the appropriate permitting authority for available records of the on-site sewage facility, often called an OSSF. Have a qualified septic professional explain the system type, condition, maintenance needs, and what the permit authorizes. Compare those records with the home as it exists today, including any additions.

A seller’s maintenance history is useful, but it does not replace a property-specific evaluation. Ask what the inspection covers, what it excludes, and whether any additional work is needed to assess the system.

Before counting on a guest house, bedroom addition, pool, or workshop, have the relevant professionals review the proposed location and use against the system layout and applicable requirements. Open land is not confirmation that an improvement can go there. Get the feasibility questions answered before treating that future project as part of the home’s value.

Put the investigations on your contract calendar

Ask your agent and inspectors to coordinate access, records, and specialist appointments as early as possible. Confirm your actual contract deadlines and what choices you have if information or an inspection will arrive late. Do not assume a general home inspection includes a complete well or septic evaluation.

If an evaluation identifies a problem, get a written scope and estimate from an appropriate professional. Then discuss the contract options with your agent, and involve your lender if the condition could affect financing. Whether a repair, credit, further investigation, or another response makes sense depends on the property and your contract.

The useful comparison is the home’s purchase price plus the work and ongoing care you are taking on. Before you decide, understand what you can document, what specialists have evaluated, and what remains uncertain.

Frequently asked questions

Does every Dripping Springs home have a well and septic system?

No. Water and wastewater arrangements vary by property. Confirm the actual services and permitting jurisdiction for the address; a Dripping Springs mailing address or ZIP code does not establish a public utility connection.

Does a home inspection cover the well and septic system?

Ask your inspector exactly what the inspection includes and excludes. Arrange qualified well, water-testing, and septic evaluations as needed rather than assuming a general home inspection covers those systems fully.

Can I add a guest house if the property already has septic?

Do not assume so. Have the permitting authority and qualified professionals review the existing permit, system capacity, site layout, and proposed use before relying on that improvement in your buying decision.

Does a passing water test mean the well will supply enough water?

A water-quality result does not establish how much water a well can reliably supply. Discuss both quality and performance with qualified professionals, including the limits of testing and the needs of your household.
